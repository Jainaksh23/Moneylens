# The Power of Compounding

**Author**: MoneyLens team  
**Last Updated**: 2026-10-15  
**Category**: Wealth Building & Mathematics  

## What is Compounding?
Compounding is the financial process where the earnings generated by an investment are reinvested to generate their own earnings over subsequent periods. Unlike simple interest—where interest accrues solely on the original principal—compound interest causes wealth to grow exponentially over time rather than linearly.

The mathematical formula for future value under compound growth:
$$A = P \times (1 + r/n)^{nt}$$
Where:
- $P$ = Initial principal sum
- $r$ = Annual nominal interest or return rate
- $n$ = Compounding frequency per year (e.g., monthly = 12)
- $t$ = Number of years invested

## The Critical Variable: Time Horizon
In compound growth, the duration of investment ($t$) exerts far greater leverage than either the initial principal ($P$) or marginal differences in annual return ($r$).
- **The Rule of 72**: A reliable mental model to calculate how many years it takes for your investment to double:
  $$\text{Years to Double} \approx \frac{72}{\text{Annual Return \%}}$$
  - At a 12% annualized return (typical historical equity index expectation), an investment doubles approximately every 6 years ($72 / 12 = 6$).
  - Over a 30-year horizon, your capital doubles five times, multiplying initial principal by $2^5 = 32\times$.

## The Cost of Delaying
Consider two individuals starting investments with 12% annualized returns:
- **Investor A** starts at age 25, investing ₹10,000 monthly for 10 years (until age 35, total investment ₹12 Lakhs), and then stops adding funds but lets the corpus compound untouched until age 60.
- **Investor B** waits until age 35 to start, investing ₹10,000 monthly continuously for 25 years (until age 60, total investment ₹30 Lakhs).
- **Outcome at Age 60**: Despite investing less than half the total capital, Investor A ends up with a significantly larger retirement corpus than Investor B because their early contributions compounded for 35 total years.

## Summary Principle
Starting early with modest sums beats starting late with large sums. Consistency and unbroken compounding are the cornerstones of long-term financial independence.
